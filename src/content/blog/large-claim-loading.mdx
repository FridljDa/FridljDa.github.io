## TL;DR
In the French motor portfolio freMTPL2, the largest 1% of claims carry 38% of the cost; the largest, 4.1 million, carries 6.9% alone. Claims like these are most likely severe bodily injuries, whose cost is set by the victim, not by the policyholder's features, and there are far too few to learn from. So every model is fitted to claims capped at 18,000, and the excess, a third of the cost, comes back as one loading $\ell = 1.51$ on every premium. That keeps the total right and the models stable, and makes $\ell$, with a 95% interval from 1.28 to 1.80, the most uncertain number in the tariff.

*Second of four posts on pricing freMTPL2, after [frequency and severity](/post/frequency-times-severity) and before [the model comparison](/post/tweedie-glm-vs-xgboost). The code and rendered reports are in [mtpl-pricing](https://github.com/FridljDa/mtpl-pricing).*

## Why cap

### Where large claims come from

Damage to a car has a ceiling; damage to a person doesn't. In France, motor liability cover for bodily injury is unlimited by law, and a severe injury can mean paying for the victim's care and lost income for the rest of their life. freMTPL2 doesn't label claim types, but car damage rarely reaches six figures, and the tail here runs to 4,075,401. The size of such a claim depends on how badly someone is hurt, not on driver age or bonus-malus, and 210 training claims above 18,000 are too few to learn where they fall.

### The problem is variance, not bias

The models are trained on a random 80% of the cleaned portfolio, 534,883 policies with 20,879 claims. The cap $c$ is the 99% quantile of their claim amounts, 18,000:

<PricingFigure name="split" />

*Training claims split at the cap: 1% of the claims lie above it, and their part above it is a third of the cost.*

Every quantity a pricing model estimates is a mean of claim amounts over some set of policies: a fold, a segment, a level of a rating factor. Where a handful of claims carry a third of the cost, that mean is set by those few amounts, and moving one of them to another set moves the estimate. Claim frequency is the same in all five cross-validation folds, between 0.073 and 0.075; the pure premium is not:

<PricingFigure name="folds" />

*Pure premium per policy-year in each fold and the test set, uncapped and capped.*

Uncapped, a model's score on a fold would depend more on which fold got the largest claims than on the model, and a relativity on where three claims fell.

### Cap each claim, load the excess

Each claim is capped on its own, $Z_{ik}^{(c)} = \min(Z_{ik}, c)$, before it is added to the policy total $S_i^{(c)}$. Every model is fitted to the capped pure premium and predicts $\hat\mu^{(c)}(x)$, and the premium charged is

$$
\hat\mu(x) = \ell\, \hat\mu^{(c)}(x), \qquad \ell = \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal I_{\mathrm{train}}} S_i}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal I_{\mathrm{train}}} S_i^{(c)}} = 1.508.
$$

If the capped model is balanced, its premiums adding up to the capped training cost, then the loaded premiums add up to the full training cost. So the cap moves cost between policies, from those that happened to have a large claim to everyone, but not out of the tariff. The question is whether it moves it fairly.

## What a flat loading assumes

Split each claim at the cap, $Z = Z^{(c)} + (Z - c)_+$, and write $m^{(c)}(x) = \mathbb E[Z^{(c)} \mid x]$ for the mean capped claim and

$$
\kappa(x) = \mathbb E[(Z - c)_+ \mid x] = \rho(x)\, \mathbb E[Z - c \mid Z > c, x], \qquad \rho(x) = \mathbb P(Z > c \mid x),
$$

for the expected excess per claim: how often a claim is large, times by how much. With claim frequency $\lambda(x)$, assume

- **(L1)** every claim carries the same expected excess, $\kappa(x) = \kappa$: a large claim is an ordinary claim that turned expensive, which can happen to anyone;
- **(L2)** the mean capped claim doesn't depend on the features, $m^{(c)}(x) = m^{(c)}$.

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu(x) &= \lambda(x)\,\big(m^{(c)}(x) + \kappa(x)\big) && \\
&= \lambda(x)\,\big(m^{(c)} + \kappa\big) && \text{(L1), (L2)} \\
&= \Big(1 + \frac{\kappa}{m^{(c)}}\Big)\, \lambda(x)\, m^{(c)} = \ell\, \mu^{(c)}(x), &&
\end{aligned}
$$

and the ratio of full to capped cost estimates exactly this $\ell$. (L2) is mild: capped claim size is close to flat across the rating factors. (L1) is the strong one, and it can be tested on the 210 training claims above the cap.

## Checking (L1)

Per rating factor, a [χ² test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-squared_test) asks whether the share of large claims is the same at every level. Most show nothing. Driver age hints at something:

<PricingFigure name="largeshare" />

*Share of training claims above the cap per driver-age band, with exact 95% intervals; dotted, the share over all claims.*

Claims of drivers up to 21 are large 2.3% of the time, 17 of 753, against 1.0% for everyone else ([Fisher's exact test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher%27s_exact_test): $p$ = 0.002). Seventeen claims are too few to price on, so the loading stays flat; with more years of data, the share of large claims per age group would be the first thing to give its own factor.

## The most uncertain number in the tariff

The excess sits in 210 claims, so $\ell$ is noisy. Resampling the training policies gives its bootstrap distribution:

<PricingFigure name="loading" />

*The large-claim loading with its 95% bootstrap interval, the value without the single largest training excess, and the value the test set would have needed.*

The single largest excess is 24% of all excess cost. The test set had only 30 policies with a claim above the cap and would have needed $\ell = 1.12$: a year with a few fewer large claims looks exactly like that. So the factor multiplying every premium is known only to within about ±20%, while the [model comparison that follows](/post/tweedie-glm-vs-xgboost) fights over fractions of a percentage point. More years of claims would improve the premium more than a better model of the capped part could.

Capping separates what the rating factors can price, how often a policy claims and what an ordinary claim costs, from what they can't: the rare claim that pays for someone's life.
