## TL;DR
In a factory's sensor data, the strongest predictor of scrap was the product type: true, already known, and not something the client can change. Instead of asking which signals predict scrap, we asked which are linked to scrap *given* the product type: is $X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z$? We answered that four ways, each testing a different consequence of the independence, and pretty consistently found no evidence that the signals affect scrap beyond the ball type. The methods' blind spots barely overlap, which is what makes the null worth something. A direct test of the independence also has two advantages over feature importance: given enough data it finds small effects, and correlated signals don't dilute it.

## The factory

On a current data science project, a client wants to know where its scrap comes from. I have changed every detail of the setting; what carries over is the structure.

A factory makes balls: footballs, volleyballs, basketballs. Every order runs through the same machines, one after the other, and each machine records its own signals: one a temperature, another a spin speed, another a pressure. Some balls come off the line below standard and are thrown away. That is the scrap.

<ScrapFigure name="line" />

*Orders of three ball types pass four machines. Each machine has a sensor; at the end, some balls are scrap.*

We look at orders, not single balls: each order has a ball type, a scrap rate, and a value for each signal over the time the order was on the line.

## The obvious answer

One thing predicted scrap well, and we found it quickly: the ball type. Basketballs are scrapped more often than footballs.

For the client, that is neither news nor help. They knew it already, and they can't act on it: an order for basketballs has to be filled with basketballs.

It also gets in the way. The machines run differently for each ball type, so nearly every signal tracks the ball type, and through it, scrap. Ask which signals predict scrap, and the data keeps answering with the ones that give away the ball type.

<ScrapFigure name="pooled" />

*Made-up orders. Across all orders, a hotter machine 2 goes with more scrap. Within each ball type, temperature says nothing about scrap: the trend across all orders is the ball type.*

## The question we asked instead

Write $Z$ for an order's ball type, $Y$ for its scrap rate, and $X_i$ for its $i$-th signal. The question is not whether $X_i$ predicts $Y$, but whether it says anything about $Y$ that $Z$ doesn't already: is the signal independent of scrap given the ball type,

$$
X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z \;?
$$

Because $Z$ takes only a few values, this reads concretely: among basketball orders, $X_i$ says nothing about scrap, and the same among footballs and among volleyballs.

<ScrapFigure name="question" />

*The ball type drives both the machine settings and the scrap rate. The question is whether a signal is linked to scrap beyond that.*

A signal for which the data rejects this independence is a candidate source of scrap. A signal for which it doesn't is, as far as the data can tell, explained by the product mix.

## What independence implies

Conditional independence is a statement about whole distributions. For every value of the signal and every ball type,

$$
p(y \mid x_i, z) = p(y \mid z).
$$

That is rarely what gets tested directly. Most methods test something it implies, usually one of these two:

1. **The best prediction doesn't change.** Once the ball type is known, knowing the signal doesn't improve the best prediction of scrap. Which prediction is best depends on the error you measure: the conditional mean for squared error, the conditional median for absolute error. For the mean:

   $$
   \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i, Z] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z].
   $$

2. **The conditional covariance is zero.** Within each ball type, signal and scrap are uncorrelated:

   $$
   \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z) = 0.
   $$

Each follows from the one before. Independence gives the first, for the mean and the median alike, since both are read off the same conditional distribution. For the second, write $\mu(Z) = \mathbb{E}[X_i \mid Z]$ and condition on $X_i$ inside:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z)
&= \mathbb{E}\big[(X_i - \mu(Z))\, Y \mid Z\big] \\
&= \mathbb{E}\big[(X_i - \mu(Z))\, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i, Z] \mid Z\big] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z]\; \mathbb{E}\big[X_i - \mu(Z) \mid Z\big] = 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Neither step reverses. Each claim is weaker than the one before it, so a test of it can pass while the signal still matters:

- **Zero covariance, but the mean moves.** Scrap often rises when a temperature drifts from its set point in either direction. Within a ball type that is a U, and if drifts both ways are about equally common, the covariance is near zero. A correlation sees nothing; the conditional mean does.
- **The same mean, but not the same distribution.** A signal can make scrap more erratic from order to order without moving its average. A comparison of mean predictions sees nothing.

*Made-up basketball orders. Scrap rises as the temperature leaves its set point either way. The best straight line is flat, so the covariance is about zero; the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i, Z]$ is not.*

## Four methods, four implications

We answered the question four ways. Each tests one implication of the independence, and that tells you what each can miss. $X$ without an index stands for all signals together.

| Method | Tests | Can miss |
|---|---|---|
| The best model we could train, explained with SHAP and permutation importance | Whether the model's predictions depend on $X_i$ | Small effects, and signals whose credit goes to correlated ones |
| Models that see only the signals, only the ball type, or both, compared on mean absolute error | $\operatorname{median}(Y \mid X, Z) = \operatorname{median}(Y \mid Z)$, for all signals at once | Which signal it is, and effects smaller than the noise between fits |
| A separate model per ball type, compared across ball types | Whether the signals predict scrap within each ball type | Ball types with few orders |
| A test of $X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z$ for each signal | $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z)] = 0$ | Links that aren't straight lines, like the U, and links of opposite sign in different ball types, which cancel |

**Feature importance** reads the first implication off one fitted model: if the independence holds, the best model has no use for $X_i$. It comes with no threshold, though, and no sense of how much evidence there is.

Permutation importance, as usually computed, shuffles $X_i$ across all orders. If the model leans on $X_i$ in place of the ball type, that shuffle also breaks the link to the ball type, and the signal looks important. Shuffling *within* each ball type keeps that link and breaks only what $X_i$ adds. If the independence holds, the shuffled orders are distributed exactly like the real ones, so repeating the shuffle turns importance into an exact test: the model's error on the real orders should look like one more draw from its errors on the shuffled ones.

**The model comparison** tests the median version of the first implication, since the prediction that minimises absolute error is the median. The comparison that tests the independence is the model with both against the one with only the ball type; the model with only the signals shows how much of the ball type they carry. All of it has to be measured on held-out orders: on the orders it was fitted to, the bigger model always fits at least as well.

**The models per ball type** condition on $Z$ by construction: each sees orders of one ball type only, so it can only find what the signals add within that type. Comparing them shows whether a signal matters for every ball type or for one.

## Testing it directly

Conditional independence is hard to test in general. [Shah and Peters (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07203) show that when $Z$ is continuous, no test can keep its false-positive rate in check for every distribution where the independence holds and still have power against any alternative. Every practical test buys its power with assumptions about how $X_i$ and $Y$ depend on $Z$.

With a handful of ball types, that problem doesn't arise: the question splits into one ordinary independence question per ball type.

The same paper proposes the test we used, the generalised covariance measure. With a categorical $Z$ it is short. For order $k$ with signal value $x_k$, scrap rate $y_k$ and ball type $z_k$, subtract the averages of its ball type, multiply, and check whether the products average to zero:

$$
R_k = \big(x_k - \bar x_{z_k}\big)\big(y_k - \bar y_{z_k}\big), \qquad T = \sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar R}{\hat\sigma_R},
$$

where $n$ is the number of orders and $\hat\sigma_R$ the standard deviation of the $R_k$. Under the independence, $T$ is approximately standard normal, so $|T| > 1.96$ rejects at the 5% level. It tests the covariance implication, which is why it shares that implication's blind spot for the U.

## Where the test and feature importance differ

### Small effects

Under a real effect, $\bar R$ settles at $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z)]$, and $T$ grows like $\sqrt{n}$ times it. Roughly, the test rejects once $\sqrt{n}$ times the effect size clears a threshold, so any conditional covariance that isn't exactly zero is found once there are enough orders.

If outside humidity raises scrap by 0.01%, the test finds it, given enough orders. Feature importance doesn't: a signal that moves every prediction by 0.01% sits at the bottom of any importance ranking, next to the signals with no effect at all, and more orders don't change that.

Whether 0.01% is worth acting on is the client's call. The test makes sure the question comes up; the effect size, reported next to the p-value, answers it.

### Correlated signals

Feature importance divides credit among the features of one model. Correlated signals, like two thermometers on one oven, share credit, and each can look less important than it is. How they share it depends on the learner: a random forest tends to spread credit over all of them, while gradient boosting and the lasso tend to pick one and leave the rest near zero. SHAP explains whichever model it is given, so it inherits the split.

A test per signal doesn't have this problem in that form. Each test conditions only on the ball type, so correlated signals don't compete for anything. The flip side is that both thermometers are flagged. Asking whether a signal adds anything beyond all the others means conditioning on them too, $X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z, X_{-i}$, and since the other signals are continuous, that brings back the hardness result above.

## What we found

Pretty consistently, a lack of evidence. Across the four methods, the measured signals showed no sign of affecting scrap beyond what the ball type already explains.

That is why there are four methods. Each has a blind spot, the last column of the table, and the blind spots barely overlap:

- Feature importance misses small effects and dilutes correlated signals; the test per signal does neither.
- The test misses links that aren't straight lines; the flexible models behind feature importance and the model comparison fit them.
- The test averages over ball types, so effects of opposite sign in different types cancel; the models per ball type look at each type alone.
- The models per ball type each see few orders; the other three use all of them.

A null from one method leaves its blind spot open. The same null from all four closes most of them.

A signal that one method flags and the others don't is weak evidence: with many signals, some reject by chance, which a false discovery rate correction such as Benjamini–Hochberg accounts for. Even a consistent flag would only make a signal a candidate, not a cause. A change of shift could move both the signal and scrap, or the signal could sit downstream of the defect: a ball damaged at machine 2 can still look different to the sensor at machine 4.

## What no evidence means

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but a null result still says something. The arithmetic that lets the test find small effects also bounds the ones it didn't find: the interval $\bar R \pm 1.96\,\hat\sigma_R/\sqrt{n}$ contains zero, and its width says how large a linear effect could still hide in the data. Feature importance gives no such bound: a signal at the bottom of the ranking may have no effect, or one too small for the model to bother with.

What the null leaves open:

- **Effects below that bound.** If one exists, more orders would find it, and the question becomes whether it is large enough to matter.
- **Causes no sensor measures.** The raw material, or handling between machines. If scrap comes from there, no analysis of the recorded signals will find it.

That makes the null a result in its own right: it points away from the signals already recorded and towards what isn't measured yet.

## The general move

The strongest predictor is often something the client already knows and can't change: the product, the site, the season. Reporting it again is easy and worthless. Condition on it, and ask what is left. Sometimes the answer is nothing the data can see, and that is worth knowing too.
