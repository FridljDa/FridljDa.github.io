## TL;DR
In a factory's sensor data, the strongest predictor of scrap was the ball type: true, already known, and not something the client can change. So instead of asking which signals predict scrap, we asked which are linked to scrap once the ball type is known. Five methods answered, and none found evidence of such a link. Each method misses different kinds of link, so together they rule out more than any one of them does. The one test per signal also bounds what it didn't find: for eleven of twelve signals, the data rule out a straight-line effect large enough to matter.

## The factory

On a current data science project, a client wants to know where its scrap comes from. To protect the client, everything in this post is fictional: the factory, the balls, the signals, and every number and figure. What carries over from the real project is the structure of the problem, the methods, and the shape of the result.

A factory makes balls: footballs, volleyballs, basketballs. Every order runs through the same four machines, M1 to M4, one after the other, and each machine records three signals, such as a temperature, a spin speed or a pressure. Some balls come off the line below standard and are thrown away. That is the scrap.

<ScrapFigure name="line" />

*Orders of three ball types $Z$ pass four machines. Each machine records three signals, twelve in all, $X_1$ to $X_{12}$. At the end, some balls are scrap.*

We look at orders, not single balls. Each order has a ball type $Z$, a scrap rate $Y$, the share of its balls thrown away, and a value for each signal $X_i$, its average over the time the order was on the line. There are $n = 400$ orders: 180 of footballs, 140 of volleyballs and 80 of basketballs. Within a ball type, the scrap rate varies from order to order with a standard deviation of about 0.6 percentage points.

## The obvious answer

One thing predicted scrap well, and we found it quickly: the ball type. Basketball orders lose 4.9% of their balls on average, footballs 1.8%.

For the client, that is neither news nor help. They knew it already, and they can't act on it: an order for basketballs has to be filled with basketballs.

It also gets in the way. The machines run differently for each ball type, so nearly every signal tracks the ball type, and through it, scrap. Ask which signals predict scrap, and the data keeps answering with the ones that give away the ball type.

<ScrapFigure name="pooled" />

*Across all orders, a hotter M2 goes with more scrap. Within each ball type, the temperature says nothing about scrap: the trend across all orders is the ball type.*

## The question we asked instead

The question is not whether $X_i$ predicts $Y$, but whether it says anything about $Y$ that $Z$ doesn't already: is the signal independent of scrap given the ball type,

$$
X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z \;?
$$

Because $Z$ takes only three values, this reads concretely: among basketball orders, $X_i$ says nothing about scrap, and the same among footballs and among volleyballs.

<ScrapFigure name="question" />

*The ball type drives both the signals and the scrap rate. The question is whether a signal is linked to scrap beyond that.*

A signal for which the data reject this independence is a candidate source of scrap, not yet a cause. A change of shift could move both the signal and scrap, or the signal could sit downstream of the defect: a ball damaged at M2 can still look different to a sensor at M4. A signal for which the data don't reject it has no link to scrap beyond the ball type that the data can detect.

## What independence implies

Conditional independence is a statement about whole distributions. For every ball type $z$, signal value $x$ and scrap rate $y$,

$$
P(Y \le y \mid X_i = x,\, Z = z) = P(Y \le y \mid Z = z).
$$

That is rarely what gets tested directly. Most methods test something it implies, usually one of these two:

1. **The best prediction doesn't change.** Once the ball type is known, knowing the signal doesn't improve the best prediction of scrap. Which prediction is best depends on the error you measure: the conditional mean minimises the expected squared error, the conditional median $\operatorname{med}(Y \mid X_i, Z)$ the expected absolute error. For the mean:

   $$
   \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i, Z] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z].
   $$

2. **The conditional covariance is zero.** Within each ball type, signal and scrap are uncorrelated:

   $$
   \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z) = 0.
   $$

Each follows from the one before. Independence gives the first, for the mean and the median alike, since both are read off the conditional distribution above. For the second, write $\mu_i(Z) = \mathbb{E}[X_i \mid Z]$, condition on $X_i$ inside, and use the first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z)
&= \mathbb{E}\big[(X_i - \mu_i(Z))\, Y \mid Z\big] \\
&= \mathbb{E}\big[(X_i - \mu_i(Z))\, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i, Z] \mid Z\big] \\
&= \mathbb{E}\big[(X_i - \mu_i(Z))\, \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z] \mid Z\big] \\
&= \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z]\; \mathbb{E}\big[X_i - \mu_i(Z) \mid Z\big] = 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Neither step reverses, so a test of either implication can pass while the signal still matters:

- **Zero covariance, but the mean moves.** Scrap often rises when a temperature drifts from its set point in either direction. Within a ball type that is a U, and if drifts both ways are about equally common, the covariance is near zero. A correlation sees nothing; the conditional mean does.
- **The same mean, but not the same distribution.** A signal can make scrap more erratic from order to order without moving its average. A comparison of mean predictions sees nothing. For the client this case matters less: the expected scrap, which is what costs money, depends on the mean alone.

<ScrapFigure name="u-shape" />

*A hypothetical signal, for basketball orders: scrap rises as the temperature leaves its set point either way. The best straight line is flat, so the covariance is about zero; the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_i,\, Z = \text{basketball}]$ is not flat.*

## Five methods and what each tests

We answered the question five ways. Write $X = (X_1, \dots, X_{12})$ for all signals together and $X_{-i}$ for all of them but $X_i$. For order $k = 1, \dots, n$, write $y_k$ for its scrap rate, $z_k$ for its ball type, and $\bar y_z$ for the average scrap rate of the orders of ball type $z$.

Every method that needed a regression model ran with three learners: XGBoost, a random forest, and [TabICL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05564), a pretrained transformer for tables that predicts from the training rows in context instead of being fitted to them.

| Method | Tests |
|---|---|
| **Feature importance:** SHAP and permutation importance of the best model of $Y$ on $(X, Z)$ | $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X, Z] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_{-i}, Z]$, a neighbouring question |
| **Model comparison:** models of $Y$ on $Z$ and on $(X, Z)$, fitted and scored on absolute error | $\operatorname{med}(Y \mid X, Z) = \operatorname{med}(Y \mid Z)$ |
| **Residual model:** a model of $Y - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z]$ on $(X, Z)$ | $\mathbb{E}\big[Y - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z] \mid X, Z\big] = 0$ |
| **Models per ball type:** a model of $Y$ on $X$ for each ball type $z$ | $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X, Z = z] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z = z]$ for each $z$ |
| **GCM:** the generalised covariance measure, for each signal | $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z)] = 0$ |

The residual model and the models per ball type test the same equation, $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X, Z] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z]$, written two ways; the model comparison tests its median version. All three take the signals together, and each holds if $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z$, which implies $X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z$ for every $i$. They differ in how they estimate the equation, and so in what they miss. Only the GCM takes one signal at a time, and feature importance answers a different question.

**Feature importance** asks whether the best of the three models uses $X_i$. For a perfect model, that is whether $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X, Z]$ changes with $X_i$ while the other signals stay fixed: whether $X_i$ adds anything to the other signals, not to the ball type alone. That is a neighbouring question, not an implication of ours, and the two differ in both directions. Two thermometers on one machine make each other redundant, so each can score low while both are linked to scrap. And a signal independent of scrap can score high: a thermometer that reads high on hot days is more accurate once the outside temperature is known, so a model uses the outside temperature, if it is recorded, even though it says nothing about scrap on its own.

Importance also comes with no threshold. Permutation importance, as usually computed, adds a third problem: it shuffles $X_i$ across all orders, so if the model leans on $X_i$ in place of the ball type, the shuffle breaks the link to the ball type and $X_i$ looks important. Shuffling within each ball type avoids that, and on held-out orders it gives a threshold. If $X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp (Y, X_{-i}) \mid Z$, the shuffled orders are distributed exactly like the real ones, so the model's error on the real orders should look like one more draw from its errors on the shuffled ones. That condition is stronger than ours, and correlated signals break it: a shuffled thermometer is paired with readings of its twin that never occur together.

**The model comparison** scores the model on $(X, Z)$ against the model on $Z$ alone, on held-out orders. Fitted to minimise absolute error, each model estimates a conditional median, so the comparison tests the median version of the first implication. Absolute error keeps a few badly scrapped orders from dominating the scores. The orders must be held out: on the orders they were fitted to, the model with more inputs looks better whether or not the inputs matter. A third model, on $X$ alone, shows how much of the ball type the signals carry.

**The residual model** removes the ball type first. With three ball types, the best prediction from the ball type alone is the average per type, so the residual $y_k - \bar y_{z_k}$ estimates $Y - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z]$. The first implication says nothing should predict it. A second model, on $(X, Z)$, tries anyway. Unlike the model comparison, it doesn't have to relearn the ball type, and it isn't judged by the difference between two noisy scores: plotted against the actual residuals of held-out orders, its predictions show the answer at a glance. Any link would tilt the cloud of orders towards the diagonal.

**The models per ball type** condition on $Z$ by construction. Each sees orders of one ball type only and is scored, on held-out orders, against that type's average scrap rate, so it can only find what the signals add within the type. Comparing them shows whether a signal matters for every ball type or for one. The price is fewer orders per model: the basketball model learns from 80.

## A test per signal

Conditional independence is hard to test in general. [Shah and Peters (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07203) show that when $Z$ is continuous, any test that keeps its false-positive rate at 5% for every distribution under which the independence holds rejects at most 5% of the time under every alternative too. Every practical test buys its power with assumptions about how $X_i$ and $Y$ depend on $Z$.

With three ball types, that problem doesn't arise: the question splits into one ordinary independence question per ball type, $X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z = z$.

The same paper proposes the test we used, the generalised covariance measure (GCM). With a categorical $Z$ it is short. Fix a signal $X_i$; to keep the formulas short, its index is dropped below. Write $x_k$ for its value in order $k$ and $\bar x_z$ for its average over the orders of ball type $z$. Subtract each order's ball-type averages, multiply, and check whether the products average to zero:

$$
R_k = \big(x_k - \bar x_{z_k}\big)\big(y_k - \bar y_{z_k}\big), \qquad T = \frac{\sqrt{n}\,\bar R}{\hat\sigma_R},
$$

where $\bar R$ and $\hat\sigma_R$ are the mean and standard deviation of $R_1, \dots, R_n$. If $X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid Z$, then for large $n$, $T$ is approximately standard normal, so its p-value is $2\,(1 - \Phi(|T|))$, with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function, and $|T| > 1.96$ rejects at the 5% level.

$\bar R$ estimates the covariance within ball types, averaged over them:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z)] = \sum_{z} P(Z = z)\, \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z = z).
$$

So the GCM tests the covariance implication, averaged over ball types. It shares that implication's blind spot for the U, and the average adds a second: covariances of opposite sign in different ball types cancel. A GCM per ball type would close the second. Compute $T_z$ from the orders of ball type $z$ alone; under the independence, $\sum_z T_z^2$ approximately follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with three degrees of freedom.

## Where the GCM and feature importance differ

### Small effects

As $n$ grows, $\bar R$ settles at $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z)]$ and $\hat\sigma_R$ at some $\sigma_R > 0$, so $|T|$ grows like $\sqrt{n}\,\big|\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, Y \mid Z)]\big| / \sigma_R$: any average covariance that isn't exactly zero is eventually found. With our 400 orders, the GCM finds a straight-line effect of about 0.09 percentage points of scrap per standard deviation of a typical signal four times out of five.

A model has a harder job with the same orders. It has to find that slope among twelve signals without knowing its shape, while the GCM estimates one number per signal. And a ranking of importances puts a small effect at the bottom, next to the signals with no effect at all, with nothing to tell them apart. The GCM reports the effect with an interval; whether an effect of that size is worth acting on is the client's call.

### Correlated signals

Feature importance divides credit among the inputs of one model. Correlated signals, like two thermometers on one machine, share it, and each can look less important than it is. How they share it depends on the learner: a random forest tends to spread credit over all of them, while gradient boosting tends to pick one and leave the rest near zero. SHAP explains whichever model it is given, so it inherits the split. This is the neighbouring question again: given one thermometer, the other adds little.

The GCM doesn't have this problem. Each test conditions only on the ball type, so correlated signals don't compete. The flip side is that both thermometers are flagged. Asking which of them matters means conditioning on the other signals too, $X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \mid (Z, X_{-i})$, and since they are continuous, that brings back the hardness result above.

## What we found

None of the five methods found evidence that a signal is linked to scrap beyond the ball type, and the three learners agreed for every method that used them.

- **Feature importance** put every signal far below the ball type, with no clear gap among the signals.
- **The model comparison**, with five-fold cross-validation, gave a mean absolute error of 0.48 percentage points with the ball type alone, 0.49 with the ball type and the signals, and 0.51 with the signals alone, varying by about 0.02 between folds. Adding the signals didn't help, and on their own they came close to the ball type because they carry it.
- **The residual model** predicted about zero for every held-out order, whatever its actual residual.
- **The models per ball type** did no better on held-out orders than each type's average scrap rate.
- **The GCM** rejected one of the twelve signals at the 5% level: $X_7$, a spin speed at M3, with $p = 0.02$. With twelve tests, 0.6 such rejections are expected by chance alone. After the Benjamini–Hochberg correction, which controls the expected share of false discoveries among the rejections, its p-value is 0.24.

<ScrapFigure name="residual" />

*Held-out orders. If the signals predicted what the ball type leaves over, the orders would tilt towards the dashed line. They lie flat: whatever the residual, the prediction stays near zero.*

A flag from one method alone deserves a look before it is dismissed, if it falls in a blind spot of the others. $X_7$'s does: a small straight-line effect is what the models miss. Its estimated effect, defined below, is 0.075 percentage points of scrap per standard deviation, with a 95% interval from 0.01 to 0.14. If it is real, it is small, and after the correction for twelve tests the data don't single it out.

### Why five methods

Each method misses some kinds of link, and a null result from one method leaves its blind spot open. Here "small" means below the noise between model fits but within the GCM's reach of about 0.09 percentage points per standard deviation:

| Kind of link | Found by |
|---|---|
| Small and straight | Only the GCM |
| Large but not straight, like the U | All but the GCM |
| Of opposite sign in different ball types | All but the GCM |
| Shared by two correlated signals | All but feature importance |
| Small and not straight | None |
| In the spread only, with the mean and median unchanged | None |

The rows the model-based methods cover assume that at least one of the three learners can fit the link's shape. Two rows stay open. A change in spread alone doesn't change the expected scrap, so we accept that gap. A link that is both small and not straight is what more orders would help every method find.

## What no evidence means

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but a null result still says something. The arithmetic that lets the GCM find small effects also bounds the effects it didn't find. $\bar R$ is a covariance, in units of the signal times percentage points, so divide it by the signal's standard deviation within ball types, $\hat s$, with $\hat s^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k} (x_k - \bar x_{z_k})^2$:

$$
\hat\beta = \frac{\bar R}{\hat s}, \qquad \hat\beta \pm 1.96\, \frac{\hat\sigma_R}{\sqrt{n}\, \hat s}.
$$

$\hat\beta$ is the slope of scrap on the signal within ball types, one slope shared by all three, times $\hat s$: by how many percentage points scrap changes when the signal moves by one standard deviation within its ball type. The interval around it is approximately a 95% confidence interval. Unlike the covariance, $\hat\beta$ is comparable across signals measured in degrees, revolutions per minute and bar.

Suppose a signal is worth acting on if one standard deviation of it moves scrap by 0.15 percentage points, about a tenth of the gap between footballs and volleyballs.

<ScrapFigure name="forest" />

*$\hat\beta$ and its 95% interval for each signal, grouped by machine. The shaded band holds effects too small to act on. $X_7$ is the one rejection before correction; $X_{11}$'s interval is wider than the band.*

Eleven of the twelve intervals lie inside the band: for those signals, the data rule out a straight-line effect large enough to matter. $X_{11}$, a pressure at M4, has a few orders with extreme values that make its interval wide, from −0.20 to 0.14, so the data can't rule out a relevant effect there. Feature importance gives no such bound: a signal at the bottom of the ranking may have no effect, or one too small for the model to pick up.

What the null leaves open:

- **$X_{11}$.** More orders, or a look at its extreme orders, would narrow its interval.
- **Small links that aren't straight lines.** The bound holds for straight-line effects only, and the models miss small effects of any shape.
- **Causes no sensor measures.** The raw material, or handling between machines. If scrap comes from there, no analysis of the recorded signals will find it.

That makes the null a result in its own right: it points away from eleven of the twelve signals and towards what isn't measured yet.

## The general move

The strongest predictor is often something the client already knows and can't change: the product, the site, the season. Reporting it again is easy and worthless. Condition on it, and ask what is left. Sometimes the answer is nothing the data can see, and that is worth knowing too.
