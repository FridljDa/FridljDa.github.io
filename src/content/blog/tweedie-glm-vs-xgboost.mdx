## TL;DR
Comparing pricing models runs into two problems. The usual metrics reward the wrong thing: absolute error is minimised by the median, zero for most policies; squared error is decided by the few largest claims; the Gini ignores the level, so a premium ten times too high scores the same. And the differences between good models are tiny, so noise decides a naive comparison. The fix is a metric minimised by the mean that weights each policy by its exposure and variance, the Tweedie deviance read as $D^2$, with calibration as a second check, and comparisons paired by fold and on the same test policies. On freMTPL2 that separates a gain of 0.4 points of $D^2$ from noise: a Poisson × gamma XGBoost beats the Tweedie GLM, and the gain holds on untouched test data.

*Third of four posts on pricing freMTPL2. The [first](/post/frequency-times-severity) splits the pure premium into frequency and severity, the [second](/post/large-claim-loading) caps large claims at 18,000; everything here is on that cleaned, capped target. The code and rendered reports are in [mtpl-pricing](https://github.com/FridljDa/mtpl-pricing).*

## The problem: most metrics reward the wrong thing

We price $\mu(x) = \mathbb E[Y \mid x, e]$, the expected claim cost per policy-year $Y = S/e$. A metric that picks the model must

- **(i)** be minimised in expectation by the true mean, so a lower score means a better premium;
- **(ii)** weight policies by the information they carry: a month of exposure says less than a year;
- **(iii)** not be decided by a few claims.

The usual suspects fail:

| Metric | Minimised by | Fails |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute error | the median, zero for most policies | (i) |
| Squared error, RMSE | the mean | (ii), (iii): every residual counts the same, though variance grows with the premium |
| Log-scale error | the geometric mean, below the mean | (i) |
| Gini of the ordered Lorenz curve | any increasing transform of the mean | (i): $\hat\mu$ and $10\,\hat\mu$ score the same |

The Gini is popular in pricing because it measures how well a tariff separates risks. But it can't tell a right premium from one ten times too high. (iii) can't be met by any metric that meets (i): the scores minimised by the mean are [exactly the Bregman divergences](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.0902), and under each of them a claim moves the comparison in proportion to its size. That is what the cap is for.

## The solution: Tweedie deviance, read as $D^2$

For a power $p \in (1, 2)$, the Tweedie unit deviance and its exposure-weighted mean over a set $A$ of held-out policies are

$$
d_p(y, \hat\mu) = 2 \left( \frac{y^{2-p}}{(1-p)(2-p)} - \frac{y\, \hat\mu^{1-p}}{1-p} + \frac{\hat\mu^{2-p}}{2-p} \right), \qquad
D_p(\hat\mu; A) = \frac{\sum_{i \in A} e_i\, d_p\big(y_i^{(c)}, \hat\mu_i\big)}{\sum_{i \in A} e_i}.
$$

- **It is minimised by the mean.** $d_p$ is a Bregman divergence, so (i) holds whatever the true distribution of $Y$.
- **It weights by variance.** Under a Tweedie model, $\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid x, e) = \phi\, \mu(x)^p / e$ and $D_p$ is the log-likelihood up to constants: each residual is divided by its variance, which is (ii). $p = 1.7$ comes from the [gamma shape of the claims](/post/frequency-times-severity); recomputed for every $p$ from 1.2 to 1.9, the ranking below doesn't change.
- **It reads as a share.** $D_p$ depends on $p$ and on the currency, so it is reported as $D^2 = 1 - D_p(\hat\mu; A) / D_p(\bar y; A)$, the share of the constant model's deviance a model explains. Models are compared by the difference of their $D^2$, not by a ratio, which would make 0.4 points look like an 11% improvement.

**Calibration, the second check.** The deviance barely sees the level. Scale the true mean by $s$: the first derivative of the expected deviance vanishes at the mean, so

$$
\mathbb E\big[d_p(Y, s\mu)\big] - \mathbb E\big[d_p(Y, \mu)\big] = (s - 1)^2\, \mu^{2-p} + O\big((s-1)^3\big).
$$

A tariff 3% too high loses almost nothing in deviance and misprices the whole portfolio by 3%. So the deviance picks the model, and calibration, total predicted over total observed, checks that the winner charges the right level.

## Telling a real gain from noise

Single-digit $D^2$ is normal for a pure premium: most of the variation is whether and how much a policy happens to claim, which no feature predicts. So good models differ by fractions of a point, and a fold with an unlucky claim is hard for every model. Two things keep that noise out:

- **Pair by fold.** For the best model $b$ and a model $m$, each fitted without fold $F_k$ and scored on it, $\Delta_k = D_p\big(\hat\mu_m^{(-k)}; F_k\big) - D_p\big(\hat\mu_b^{(-k)}; F_k\big)$ and $\mathrm{SE} = s_\Delta / \sqrt K$. The fold's luck cancels in the difference. Then the [one-standard-error rule](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781315139470): take the simplest model within one SE of the best, because simpler models are easier to explain and to file with a regulator.
- **Pair on the test set.** The winner and the reference are refitted on all training policies and scored once on test policies set aside before anything was fitted. A bootstrap that resamples the same policies for both models gives an interval for their difference, not just for each.

## The result, briefly

Six models, each predicting the capped pure premium: a constant, a Tweedie GLM with splines and ridge penalty (the industry standard), Poisson × gamma GLMs, the same two set-ups with [XGBoost](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/) trees, monotone in bonus-malus, and the tabular foundation model [TabICL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11139).

<PricingFigure name="gains" />

*Each model's $D^2$ minus the Tweedie GLM's on the same held-out fold (dots) and the mean (bar), in percentage points.*

- **Cross-validation.** Poisson × gamma XGBoost explains 4.54% of the deviance, the Tweedie GLM 4.10%. Paired by fold, the GLM is 5.3 standard errors behind.
- **Test set.** On 133,721 untouched policies the gain is 0.42 points, with a 95% interval from 0.23 to 0.61, positive in all 200 resamples. The two models' own intervals overlap; their difference doesn't, because both are scored on the same policies.
- **Calibration.** The winner predicts 1.017 times the test set's capped cost, the constant model 1.038: the test policies simply claimed less.
- **TabICL** trails at 2.94%: it sees only 10,000 policies as context. On the same 10,000, it gets 92% of the trees' $D^2$.

## What the winner learned

Trees have no coefficients, so the model is read through [partial dependence](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1013203451): set one feature to a value for every policy, average the premium, divide by the average premium. For a multiplicative model that is the relativity curve of a tariff.

<PricingFigure name="relativities" />

*Partial dependence on bonus-malus and driver age, relative to the average premium; each panel has its own scale.*

- **Bonus-malus is the tariff.** From 50 to 106 the premium rises by a factor of 6.7, almost all of it claim frequency. Bonus-malus moves only with the policyholder's own claims, so it measures what no other factor can.
- **Driver age is not U-shaped.** It falls from 1.14 at 20 to 0.75 near 28 and rises to 1.24 near 49, which fits parents whose children drive the car too.

The gain is small and real, and what it costs is a tariff that can be filed line by line. So the Tweedie GLM stays as the reference, and the next step is a GLM with the interactions the trees find, such as driver age with bonus-malus. The largest uncertainty in the premium isn't in this comparison at all: it is the [large-claim loading](/post/large-claim-loading), known only to within about ±20%.
