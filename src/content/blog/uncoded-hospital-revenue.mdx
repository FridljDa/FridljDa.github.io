## TL;DR
German hospitals are paid per case, by codes, so care that was given and documented but never coded is never paid for. Malnutrition is the classic example: the nurses screen for it, a dietitian treats it, and because no physician ever writes the diagnosis down, nobody codes it. In the system I'd build to find such gaps, no model decides on its own. It works on a periodic copy of the hospital's data, never on the production KIS. Cheap signals in code raise candidate codes, and a self-hosted LLM agent checks each one with four read-only tools: the record of one case and no other, the code catalog, Python for the arithmetic, and the hospital's past coding decisions. Its answer is typed, so it can only name a code that exists and cite a passage that is in the record. A certified grouper prices it, and a coder decides. Both kinds of error cost money, but only one can be measured: the share of suggestions coders accept, the precision. So that is what the system optimises. At a few hundred discharges a day even in a large hospital, throughput is not the problem; getting data out of each hospital, earning coders' trust and evaluating without ground truth are.

## How a German hospital gets paid

You can't design a system for a domain you can't draw, so first the chain the system has to fit into.

<DesignFigure name="billing-chain" />

*The six stations of an inpatient case's billing. Grey belongs to the hospital, teal is the coders, orange the payer. The gap this post is about sits between documenting and coding.*

### From the record to a DRG

A hospital is paid per inpatient case with a flat rate, the DRG (diagnosis-related group). Physicians and nurses document the treatment in the hospital information system, the KIS (*Krankenhausinformationssystem*). Coders turn that record into codes: [ICD-10-GM](https://www.bfarm.de/DE/Kodiersysteme/Klassifikationen/ICD/ICD-10-GM/_node.html) for diagnoses, one main diagnosis and any number of secondary ones, and [OPS](https://www.bfarm.de/DE/Kodiersysteme/Klassifikationen/OPS-ICHI/OPS/_node.html) (*Operationen- und Prozedurenschlüssel*) for procedures.

The rulebook for coding is the [DKR](https://www.gkv-spitzenverband.de/media/dokumente/krankenversicherung_1/krankenhaeuser/drg/drg_2026/DKR_2026_20.11.2025.pdf) (*Deutsche Kodierrichtlinien*), the German coding guidelines. A certified grouper, deterministic software built on the [catalog](https://www.g-drg.de/content/download/17468/file/Fallpauschalenkatalog_2026_2025-11-19.pdf) that the InEK (*Institut für das Entgeltsystem im Krankenhaus*) publishes every year, then maps the codes plus age, length of stay and a few more fields to a DRG. The DRG part of the invoice is roughly

$$
\text{DRG revenue} \approx \text{cost weight} \times \text{base rate},
$$

with a [state base rate](https://www.vdek.com/vertragspartner/Krankenhaeuser/landesbasisfallwerte/_jcr_content/par/download_986753009/file.res/LBFW_2026_Final_2026.05.28.pdf) of about 4,570 EUR in 2026; nursing staff costs are [paid separately, per day](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/khentgg/__6a.html).

### Why one secondary diagnosis matters

Secondary diagnoses matter more than you'd think. They feed a severity score, the PCCL (patient clinical complexity level, 0 to 6), which often decides the DRG's suffix letter: F62B, heart failure with higher severity, pays more than F62C. But under DKR rule D003, a secondary diagnosis only counts if it caused treatment, diagnostics, or extra care or monitoring. What a counted code is worth follows from grouping the case twice:

$$
\text{price of a code} = \big(w_{\text{with}} - w_{\text{without}}\big) \times \text{base rate},
$$

where $w$ is the cost weight of the DRG the case lands in with and without the code.

<DesignFigure name="pricing" />

*The price of one code: group the same case without it and with it. Here acute kidney failure raises the severity enough to move the case from F62C to F62B, and the formula above prices the move.*

### Audits

The invoice goes to the patient's *Krankenkasse*, the statutory health insurer, which can send it to the MD (*Medizinischer Dienst*) for audit. Since 2021 the MD has been [formally independent of the insurers](https://md-bund.de/ueber-uns/die-medizinischen-dienste-und-der-medizinische-dienst-bund.html), though they still fund it. The MD cuts whatever the record doesn't support. How many of a hospital's invoices a payer may send to audit depends on its track record. With $s$ the share of its audited invoices the MD left uncut last time, the [cap until the end of 2026](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/sgb_5/__275c.html) is

$$
\text{audit quota} \le \begin{cases} 5\,\% & \text{if } s \ge 60\,\% \\ 10\,\% & \text{if } 40\,\% \le s < 60\,\% \\ 15\,\% & \text{if } s < 40\,\%. \end{cases}
$$

A [law passed in July 2026](https://www.bundesgesundheitsministerium.de/ministerium/meldungen/bundestag-beschliesst-gkv-beitragssatzstabilisierunggesetz-pm-10-07-2026) raises the cap to 40 percent from 2027, and removes it entirely for the hospitals with the worst track record. With [two thirds of hospitals](https://www.dki.de/pressemitteilung/dkg-zum-dki-krankenhaus-barometer) posting a loss in 2024, two things matter at once: finding the revenue that was earned but not coded, and never claiming a code the record can't defend.

Both halves are errors with a price. A false negative, a code the record supports but nobody claims, is revenue lost for good once the case is billed. A false positive, a code the record can't defend, is paid back after an audit, and a worse track record lets the payers audit more of every future invoice. A system that only maximises revenue is an upcoding machine, and stricter audits punish exactly that.

## What the system must do

<DesignMap preset="clarify" />

*All we know so far: documentation goes in, and a coder should get something to act on.*

### The running example

The running example is invented; the coding rules around it are real. A 74-year-old is admitted with heart failure, and two things happen that nobody codes:

- **Acute kidney failure.** On day three, creatinine is 4.3 mg/dl against 1.2 on admission, $4.3 / 1.2 \approx 3.6$ times the baseline, above the threefold rise that defines [KDIGO stage 3](https://doi.org/10.1038/kisup.2012.1). The ward pauses the ACE inhibitor and checks creatinine daily. That is treatment under D003, so [N17.93](https://gesund.bund.de/icd-code-suche/n17-93) counts, and it moves the case from F62C to F62B, worth roughly a thousand euros in this invented case.
- **Malnutrition.** The nursing admission screening, the [NRS 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0261-5614(02)00214-5) (Nutritional Risk Screening), scores 4, where 3 already means the patient is at risk. A dietitian's note on day four records a 10 percent weight loss in three months and prescribes supplement drinks twice a day. No physician mentions it. The coding guidelines make diagnoses the treating physician's responsibility, so a coder won't code what only a dietitian wrote down, and [E44.0](https://gesund.bund.de/icd-code-suche/e44-0), moderate malnutrition, stays uncoded.

<DesignFigure name="running-example" />

*The two misses are different kinds of evidence. The kidney failure is a number crossing a line, which a rule can watch. The malnutrition is spread over documents that have to be read, and the physician's diagnosis is missing. Both were treated; only one has a physician behind it.*

The second is the kind a rule can't find: scattered across scanned nursing notes, worded differently every time, and blocked on a physician's confirmation. A design that handles both is the goal.

### Boundaries

Before drawing anything, the problem needs boundaries. These are the ones I'd settle on:

- **Coders act on the output.** They own the codes, so they get the suggestions. Physicians are the source of missing facts, and controlling, the finance department, only sees totals.
- **Review runs while the patient is admitted and settles by discharge.** Reviewing early leaves time to ask a physician while they still remember the case; reviewing late gives a more complete record. Reviewing during the stay gets both.
- **It doesn't need to be live.** Analytical work does not belong on the database that serves operations, the point *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* makes about separating the two: a heavy read at the wrong moment slows the system that physicians and nurses are working in, and a hospital's IT department is right to be nervous about that. Nothing here needs data from the last minute either; a few refreshes a day settle a case long before discharge. So the system reads a copy, refreshed periodically, in whatever store the hospital's IT is already comfortable running. That also makes a new hospital easier to win: a read-only export is a much smaller ask than access to the production KIS.
- **Both errors cost money, but only one can be measured.** A missed code costs its revenue delta, and two thirds of hospitals are losing money. A wrong code is paid back after an audit, and a bad audit record raises the audit rate for every case. Of the two, only wrong codes show up in our data: a coder rejects a suggestion, or the MD cuts an accepted one months later. A missed code shows up nowhere, since nobody reviews the cases we said nothing about. So the system optimises for precision, the share of its suggestions that hold up, and finds as much as it can without letting precision drop (more in the evaluation below).
- **The data is heterogeneous.** Structured data from the KIS comes as [HL7 v2](https://www.hl7.org/implement/standards/product_brief.cfm?product_id=185) messages, the decades-old workhorse of hospital data exchange, or through [FHIR](https://hl7.org/fhir/), the newer web-API standard from the same organisation. Alongside it come discharge letters, lab PDFs, scanned nursing notes, in a different mix at every hospital.
- **Patient data stays in Germany.** German law [keeps cloud processing of health data](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/sgb_5/__393.html) in the EU or in countries with an adequacy decision, and hospitals' own rules go further, so in practice that means German data centers, and a model that runs there too.
- **Scale is small.** Even a large university hospital discharges only a few hundred inpatients a day, around [150,000 a year](https://www.charite.de/service/pressemitteilung/artikel/detail/charite_bilanz_2025_deutliches_plus_bei_patientinnen_und_forschung); the [average German hospital](https://www.destatis.de/DE/Themen/Gesellschaft-Umwelt/Gesundheit/Krankenhauser/Tabellen/gd-krankenhaeuser-jahre.html) handles about 10,000 cases a year. To size the model's load, take a large hospital and two guesses of mine, not measurements: about 2,000 patients admitted at any time (300 discharges a day at a stay of about a week), and one agent run per case per day, for the cases whose record changed, each reading about 50,000 tokens through its tools. That is $2{,}000 \times 50{,}000 = 100$ million tokens a day, mostly input. For a self-hosted open-weight LLM that is a few GPUs: a real cost, but small next to the roughly thousand euros one found code can be worth. Cost is not what limits the design; precision is. Even a few dozen such hospitals are a small load for one database.

The last point shapes the design. The hard part is not requests per second. It is getting data out of a dozen KIS installations without touching what runs the wards, keeping the view of a case current as the record changes, and making coders trust suggestions enough to act on them.

## The simplest design that works

<DesignMap preset="end-to-end" />

*Six steps, still technology-agnostic. Blue boxes are the platform, the teal one is a person, the grey one belongs to the hospital.*

End to end:

1. **Hospital systems** hand over a periodic copy of what happened to each case.
2. A **case context store** keeps one record per case: every code, document and lab value, with the time it arrived.
3. **Gap detectors** read a case and propose codes it is missing, each with the evidence for it.
4. A **grouper** prices each proposed code: it groups the case with and without it and reports the revenue delta.
5. A **ranked worklist** shows the coder the suggestions worth their time, best first. They accept or reject.
6. **Write-back and labels:** accepted codes go back into the KIS, and every decision goes back to the detectors as a label.

Two things already fall out of this. Each suggestion points at a case *version*, the record as it stood when the suggestion was made, so when the record changes we know which suggestions to re-check. And the grouper sits after the detectors, not inside them: the model confirms, deterministic certified software does the money math. I never want a model to tell a coder what a code is worth.

### What runs when

Nothing in the pipeline waits for a person, and no person waits for the pipeline. The suggestion queue sits between them.

<DesignFigure name="lanes" />

*The background keeps the queue current; the foreground only reads and decides.*

- **In the background, asynchronously:** each sync from the hospital's copy, a few times a day, runs the detectors and the grouper on the cases that changed and updates their suggestions in the queue, hours behind the record. Physician answers come back as new events, often days later. A new catalog year re-runs the open cases.
- **In the foreground, synchronously:** a coder opening the queue gets suggestions that are already computed, in milliseconds. Accept and reject are saved at once; writing a code back to the KIS is queued.
- **The deadline is discharge.** A case's suggestions should settle before it is billed, so cases close to discharge rank first and a final run happens on the first sync after discharge.

## The components

<DesignMap preset="components" />

*The same six steps, each with the components that make it concrete. Each part below highlights its step.*

### Ingestion and case store

<DesignMap preset="store" />

- **A copy, not the KIS:** the hospital's IT exports what changed since the last sync, a few times a day, into a store they are comfortable with: a reporting replica, a data warehouse they already run, or files dropped from their interface engine, which already archives the HL7 v2 and FHIR traffic. We read that copy and never query the production KIS.
- **Adapters:** one per KIS export, turning those rows, messages and files into events on one timeline per case: admission, transfer, lab result, document, discharge. Scans go through OCR. Lab results, for example, come with local test codes and units (creatinine in mg/dl or µmol/l) that are mapped once per hospital.
- **Where the effort goes:** records get corrected or cancelled after the fact, so an adapter applies corrections instead of appending. And every hospital's installation is configured differently.
- **Store:** Postgres, an append-only event log per case plus a materialised current view. The log gives versions and a full change history for free, and a few hundred thousand cases a year need no sharding.
- **Orchestration:** each sync starts a durable workflow per changed case, for example in [Temporal](/post/temporal-human-in-the-loop), keyed by case and version. A sync processed twice runs once, and a question to a physician can wait days without a process waiting with it. Ten lab results between two syncs cause one re-run, and every result is cached under $\text{hash}(\text{inputs}, \text{catalog year}, \text{detector version})$, so a re-run only pays for what changed.

<DesignFigure name="event-log" />

*The running example's case in the store. Every change a sync brings is a new version; nothing is overwritten. The suggestion points at the version it was made on, so when the lab corrects a value two versions later, the store knows which suggestion to re-check. 4.1 is still over three times the baseline, so it stands.*

### Gap detectors

<DesignMap preset="detect" />

A detector is code, versioned and tested like any other: a cheap signal that raises a candidate code, the fields the coding agent has to fill in about it, and the DKR rules it has to pass. A few examples, with illustrative thresholds; the real ones come from the coding guidelines and each hospital's coders:

| Signal | Candidate code | Treatment that would satisfy D003 |
|---|---|---|
| Creatinine at least 3 × the admission value | N17.93, acute kidney failure, stage 3 | a drug paused, fluids, daily checks |
| NRS 2002 score of 3 or more | E43 / E44.0 / E44.1, malnutrition by severity | a dietitian, supplement drinks |
| Potassium below 3.5 mmol/l twice | E87.6, hypokalaemia | potassium substitution |
| A procedure code without its usual diagnosis | the diagnosis the procedure implies | the procedure itself |

#### Signals and the coding agent

- **Cheap signals,** to decide where to look: thresholds on lab values, screening scores, the medication list, procedure codes. Code, fast, run on every version.
- **Coding agent,** for precision: an LLM agent that reads the case through four read-only tools and answers in a typed record. The documents are split into numbered passages after OCR, so the agent cites passages by number.
- **Rule checks:** the mechanical DKR checks. Is this code combination valid, and does D003 hold given what the evidence says was done?

The agent's tools:

- **Case reader:** browse and search the copy of the hospital's data, documents, lab values, medication. It is bound to one patient and one case when the run starts, in code, not by the prompt: the model never passes a case ID, so nothing it writes can open another patient's record. That is data protection, and it removes a whole class of error, facts from the wrong patient, deterministically.
- **ICD-10-GM catalog:** the exact codes of the case's catalog year, with what the catalog says about each one, its inclusions, exclusions and notes, and the DKR rules that apply. That is what tells E44.0 from E43.
- **Python:** a sandbox with no network that sees only what the case reader returned. LLMs are unreliable at arithmetic, and coding is full of it: is 4.3 more than three times 1.2, how many kilograms is 10 percent in three months? Python computes it; the model only decides what to compute.
- **Past decisions:** how this hospital's coders decided on earlier cases, which suggestions they accepted and rejected and which codes the cases were finally billed with. That shows the agent the mapping from record to code by example, and each hospital's house rules with it, without anyone configuring them by hand. Every way the system adapts to a hospital on its own is one less thing to set up per hospital.

The answer is a [Pydantic](/post/pydantic-ai-type-safe-hybrid-workflows) model, not free text: the code must exist in the catalog of the case's year, every cited passage must exist in this case, and the yes-or-no fields are booleans. An answer that fails validation goes back to the agent once and is then dropped, so nothing invalid ever reaches a coder. A citation that has to be a passage number can't point at a quote that isn't there, which is a better story for an audit than checking generated quotes afterwards.

#### Traps

A keyword finds malnutrition in many passages that don't prove it. Clinical text is full of negations, earlier stays, plans and text copied forward from old letters, and these are exactly the passages the agent must not cite:

<DesignFigure name="traps" />

*Five passages a search for malnutrition returns. Only the last proves current, treated malnutrition. The traps are what the labelled test set for a detector has to cover.*

#### Shipping a detector

- **Tests:** a few hundred coder-labelled candidates, traps included, run on every change.
- **Versions:** each detector carries a version and a catalog year, so results stay reproducible and cache correctly.
- **Shadow mode first:** a new detector or version logs what it would suggest on live cases before any coder sees it (see the evaluation below).

### Grouper

<DesignMap preset="grouper" />

The certified grouper as a service: case in, DRG and revenue out, deterministic, milliseconds per run. It prices a suggestion the way the pricing figure above shows, by grouping the case with and without the code.

### Worklist

<DesignMap preset="worklist" />

Suggestions are ranked by expected value, with $P(\text{coder accepts})$ estimated from the hospital's past decisions on similar suggestions:

$$
\text{score} = \Delta\text{revenue} \times P(\text{coder accepts}) - \lambda \times \text{audit risk}
$$

A code with $\Delta\text{revenue} = 0$ still appears, ranked last, since the guidelines ask for every diagnosis that meets their definition.

<DesignFigure name="ranking" />

*Three invented suggestions. The most valuable code ranks second: its evidence is thin, so the coder is unlikely to accept it and an audit likely to cut it.*

- **Evidence on screen:** the quote highlighted on its page, the DRG before and after, one-click reject reasons.
- **Questions bundled:** a suggestion that needs a fact only the physician can supply becomes a question, one yes or no per case, not six interruptions.

<DesignFigure name="worklist" />

*What the coder sees for the running example: the kidney failure as a suggestion, with the evidence highlighted and the DRG before and after, and the malnutrition as a question for the physician.*

One click on a quote opens its page, so the coder checks the evidence where it was written, not a copy of it:

### Write-back and labels

<DesignMap preset="feedback" />

- **Write-back:** accepted codes go into the KIS through the same adapter, so the hospital's system stays the system of record. Many installations don't allow writing codes back; there the first version is a coder copying the code, and that is fine.
- **Labels:** every decision goes to a labels table the evaluation reads.

## The life of one suggestion

<DesignMap preset="suggestion" />

*Highlighted: the part where the model, the rules and the money meet.*

The kidney failure takes the easy path: the creatinine threshold fires, the agent checks the ratio in Python and cites the lab report and the note pausing the ACE inhibitor, D003 holds, and the grouper prices the move to F62B. It becomes a suggestion in the cardiology coder's queue. The malnutrition is harder, and more typical:

<DesignFigure name="suggestion" />

*The malnutrition case, from the signal that raised it to a question for the physician. Dropping is dashed: it is the outcome nobody sees.*

The screening score of 4 is the cheap signal. The agent reads the scanned nursing notes, looks up E44.0 and its neighbours in the catalog to match the severity, and cites the dietitian's note on page 1, with the weight loss and the supplement drinks. The rule check says D003 holds, since the malnutrition was treated, but finds no physician's diagnosis, which a coder needs. So it doesn't become a suggestion. It becomes one bundled question to the physician:

<DesignFigure name="physician-question" />

*The physician's side: questions from coding, one card per case, each with the evidence attached and a one-click answer.*

If the physician confirms, the code re-enters the queue as a suggestion, priced like any other. Had the signal been a single low potassium value, normal on the recheck and never treated, D003 would fail and the candidate would be dropped.

### Tradeoffs

- **Rules, a model, or both.** Rules alone are precise but brittle: the creatinine rule works, but nobody writes a rule for every way a nurse records weight loss. A model alone needs a question to answer, and asking it about every possible code on every case buries the coder in low-confidence guesses. The hybrid uses rules to say *where to look* and the model to *read what's there*. That matches [a support automation I built](/post/first-level-support-automation): code in charge of the sequence and the model confined to interpretation made each piece testable on its own.
- **Which model.** An open-weight LLM, self-hosted in a German data center, measured on a few hundred coder-labelled candidates before it is trusted. The cheaper option is a non-generative decision model that only picks from fixed options, the kind TypeSafe [introduced with Jev](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev). I'd pass on it: it can't use tools, so it can't look up the catalog, compute a ratio or check what the hospital decided before, and the saving is small next to what one wrong code costs. The typed answer makes the model a swap, not a redesign.
- **Precision first.** A coder who sees three bad suggestions in a row stops trusting the queue, and then the good ones go unread too. I'd start with a few detectors that are nearly always right, and add more only when the acceptance rate holds.
- **Workflow and agent.** The pipeline is a [workflow](/post/workflow-vs-agent): every suggestion goes through the same checks in the same order, and the order is what makes it defensible in an audit. The agent sits inside one step of it, the one that has to read, where its freedom to browse pays off, and its tools are read-only, so the worst it can do is give an answer the checks after it reject.
- **Live or periodic.** Reading the production KIS directly would make suggestions minutes fresher, at the price of load on the system the wards depend on and a far harder conversation with every hospital's IT. Hours of delay cost nothing when the deadline is discharge.

When to upgrade: only once precision holds and coders still trust the queue would I let the agent look for codes no signal raised.

## Running it: evaluation, rollout, security

<DesignMap preset="finishing" />

*Highlighted: the decisions, which close the loop into the evaluation.*

### Evaluation

Measure only what the flow actually produces. Coders review our suggestions, not the gaps we stayed quiet about, so every suggestion gets a verdict and every miss stays invisible: a code we never suggested is never reviewed, and the case is billed without it. That makes precision measurable and recall not, so the evaluation is built on precision and doesn't pretend otherwise.

- **Precision:** the share of suggestions coders accept, per detector and per hospital. Cheap, fast and plentiful, but biased by whatever the queue showed them.
- **Ground truth for precision:** MD audit outcomes, months later and only for the [fewer than one in ten cases](https://md-bund.de/statistik/gkv-beratungen-und-begutachtungen.html) that get audited today. They catch the accepted codes that still didn't hold up.
- **The metric:** not the acceptance rate alone but $\sum_{\text{accepted}} \Delta\text{revenue} - \text{euros cut in audits}$, the same gap as [between an approval rate and savings](/post/approval-rate-is-not-savings). Both terms are in our data.
- **Reviewers:** coders disagree with each other, so I'd measure each one's agreement with the others before trusting their labels.
- **Shadow mode, not replay:** offline replay is weaker than it looks, because it is hard to recreate what the record looked like when a coder would have seen the suggestion. The honest test runs a new detector on live cases, logs what it would have suggested, and has a coder check a sample, which gives its precision before it goes live. [The Missing Test Suite](/post/the-missing-test-suite) makes the longer case for that, and for why the experts' review throughput is the real bottleneck.

*A new detector version in shadow mode next to the live one. The numbers are invented. It suggests more at a slightly lower precision, which is the trade to decide on before promoting it; the audit outcomes that settle it arrive months later.*

### Adding a hospital

Adding a hospital takes three things: a periodic export its IT is comfortable with plus an adapter for it (or a configuration of an existing one), a backfill of open cases, and the hospital's past coding decisions loaded for the agent. Those decisions carry most of the house rules, so the per-hospital overlay of rules on top of the shared DKR rules stays small. The yearly catalog updates for ICD-10-GM, OPS and the DRG catalog mean every rule, signal and grouper run is tied to a catalog year, versioned and tested like code. A case admitted in December and discharged in January is the edge case to test first.

### Security

German hosting for data and model (see the boundaries above), encryption, per-hospital isolation, read-only access to a copy instead of the production KIS, an agent that can see one case per run and no other, and the case store's change history, which shows which data the agent saw for each suggestion.

### Contracts and growth

Three small records are the contracts between the parts: a case version (the record as of one change), a suggestion (the proposed code, its evidence, the rule that allows it, the revenue delta and a status), and a coder's decision with its reason. Because only these cross boundaries, ingestion, detectors, the grouper service and the review UI can change independently. They also carry the next products: answering the MD's audit queries from the same evidence, billing privately insured patients under the GOÄ (*Gebührenordnung für Ärzte*), the physicians' fee schedule, and showing controlling the revenue at risk.
