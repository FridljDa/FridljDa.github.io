## TL;DR
German hospitals are paid per case, by codes, so care that was given and documented but never coded is never paid for. Malnutrition is the classic example: the nurses screen for it, a dietitian treats it, and because no physician ever writes the diagnosis down, nobody codes it. In a system that finds such gaps, no model decides anything on its own: cheap signals in code raise candidate codes, a self-hosted decision model that cannot write text confirms each one by picking the passage that proves it, a certified grouper prices it, and a coder decides. It has to favour precision over recall, because a wrong code can fail an audit, and a coder who sees a few bad suggestions stops trusting the queue. At a few hundred discharges a day per hospital, throughput is not the problem; integrating with each hospital's systems, earning coders' trust and evaluating without ground truth are.

## How a German hospital gets paid

You can't design a system for a domain you can't draw, so first the chain the system has to fit into.

<DesignFigure name="billing-chain" />

*The six stations of an inpatient case's billing. Grey belongs to the hospital, teal is the coders, orange the payer. The gap this post is about sits between documenting and coding.*

A hospital is paid per inpatient case with a flat rate, the DRG (diagnosis-related group). Physicians and nurses document the treatment in the hospital information system, the KIS (*Krankenhausinformationssystem*). Coders turn that record into codes: ICD-10-GM for diagnoses, one main diagnosis and any number of secondary ones, and OPS (*Operationen- und Prozedurenschlüssel*) for procedures.

The rulebook for coding is the DKR (*Deutsche Kodierrichtlinien*), the German coding guidelines. A certified grouper, deterministic software built on the catalog that the InEK (*Institut für das Entgeltsystem im Krankenhaus*) publishes every year, then maps the codes plus age, length of stay and a few more fields to a DRG. The DRG part of the invoice is roughly the DRG's cost weight times a state base rate of 4,000 to 5,000 EUR; nursing staff costs are paid separately, per day.

Secondary diagnoses matter more than you'd think. They feed a severity score, the PCCL (patient clinical complexity level, 0 to 6), which often decides the DRG's suffix letter: F62B, heart failure with higher severity, pays more than F62C. But under DKR rule D003, a secondary diagnosis only counts if it caused treatment, diagnostics, or extra care or monitoring.

The invoice goes to the patient's *Krankenkasse*, the statutory health insurer, which can send it to the MD (*Medizinischer Dienst*) for audit. The MD cuts whatever the record doesn't support. How many of a hospital's invoices a payer may send to audit depends on its track record: the larger the share of its audited invoices the MD cut last time, the more of its invoices may be audited next. Today that share is capped at [5 to 15 percent](https://www.mydrg.de/myDRG_archives/diagnosis-related-groups-drg/weitere-verschaerfung-bei-den-pruefquoten.html). A reform going through the Bundestag in 2026 would raise the cap to 40 percent, and remove it entirely for the hospitals with the worst track record; the exact thresholds have shifted between drafts. With [two thirds of hospitals](https://www.aerzteblatt.de/news/zwei-von-drei-krankenhausern-haben-rote-zahlen-geschrieben-d29a94f8-a5b8-4627-b56a-bc1182b9a4b2) posting a loss in 2024, two things matter at once: finding the revenue that was earned but not coded, and never claiming a code the record can't defend.

That second half is the one this design keeps coming back to. A system that only maximises revenue is an upcoding machine, and stricter audits punish exactly that.

## What the system must do

<DesignMap preset="clarify" />

*All we know so far: documentation goes in, and a coder should get something to act on.*

The running example is invented; the coding rules around it are real. A 74-year-old is admitted with heart failure, and two things happen that nobody codes:

- **Acute kidney failure.** On day three, creatinine is 4.3 mg/dl against 1.2 on admission, more than three times the baseline: stage 3. The ward pauses the ACE inhibitor and checks creatinine daily. That is treatment under D003, so N17.93 counts, and it moves the case from F62C to F62B, worth roughly a thousand euros in this invented case.
- **Malnutrition.** The nursing admission screening, the NRS 2002 (Nutritional Risk Screening), scores 4. A dietitian's note on day four records a 10 percent weight loss in three months and prescribes supplement drinks twice a day. No physician mentions it. A coder can't code a diagnosis no physician made, so E44.0, moderate malnutrition, stays uncoded.

The first is easy to find with a lab threshold. The second is the kind a rule can't find: scattered across scanned nursing notes, worded differently every time, and blocked on a physician's confirmation. A design that handles both is the goal.

Before drawing anything, the problem needs boundaries. These are the ones I'd settle on:

- **Coders act on the output.** They own the codes, so they get the suggestions. Physicians are the source of missing facts, and controlling only sees totals.
- **Review runs while the patient is admitted and settles by discharge.** Earlier leaves time to ask a physician while they still remember the case; later gives a more complete record. Concurrent review gets both.
- **A wrong code costs more than a missed one.** A missed code costs its revenue delta once. A wrong code can fail an audit, and a bad audit record raises the audit rate for every case. So the system optimises for precision.
- **The data is heterogeneous.** Structured data from the KIS over the HL7 v2 or FHIR standards, discharge letters, lab PDFs, scanned nursing notes, in a different mix at every hospital.
- **Patient data stays in Germany.** GDPR and the hospitals' own rules mean German data centers, and a model that runs there too.
- **Scale is small.** A hospital discharges a few hundred inpatients a day. If each case changes about five times and raises about three candidates, that is around 4,500 model calls a day per hospital, each over about 20,000 tokens of documentation: some 90 million tokens a day. For a generative LLM that is real money. For a small decision model on one GPU, it is not, so cost is not what limits the design; precision is. For a database, a few dozen hospitals are nothing.

The last point shapes the design. The hard part is not requests per second. It is getting data out of a dozen KIS installations, keeping the view of a case current as the record changes, and making coders trust suggestions enough to act on them.

## The simplest design that works

<DesignMap preset="end-to-end" />

*Six steps, still technology-agnostic. Blue boxes are the platform, the teal one is a person, the grey one belongs to the hospital.*

The simplest design that works end to end:

1. **Hospital systems** send what happens to a case.
2. A **case context store** keeps one record per case: every code, document and lab value, with the time it arrived.
3. **Gap detectors** read a case and propose codes it is missing, each with the evidence for it.
4. A **counterfactual grouper** runs the case with and without each proposed code and reports the revenue delta.
5. A **ranked worklist** shows the coder the suggestions worth their time, best first. They accept or reject.
6. **Write-back and labels:** accepted codes go back into the KIS, and every decision goes back to the detectors as a label.

The data between the steps is small and worth naming, because it is the interface teams would build against:

- a **case version**: case ID, hospital, version number, codes, documents and lab values as of that version;
- a **suggestion**: case ID and version, proposed code, evidence (document, page, quote), the rule that allows it, the revenue delta, and a status (open, accepted, rejected, asked);
- a **decision**: suggestion ID, who, accept or reject, and a reason from a short list.

Two things already fall out of this. Suggestions point at a case *version*, so when the record changes we know which suggestions to re-check. And the grouper sits after the detectors, not inside them: the model confirms, deterministic certified software does the money math. I never want a model to tell a coder what a code is worth.

## The components

<DesignMap preset="components" />

*The same six steps, each with the components that make it concrete.*

**Ingestion.** One adapter per KIS, translating HL7 v2 messages, FHIR resources or file drops into events on one internal case timeline: admission, transfer, new lab result, new document, discharge. Scans go through OCR. Two gotchas make this the component I'd expect to eat the most engineering time. Messages arrive out of order and get corrected or cancelled afterwards, so the adapter has to apply corrections rather than just append. And every hospital's installation is configured differently.

**Case store.** Postgres, with an append-only events table per case and a materialised current view. The log gives versioning and the audit log for free: for every suggestion we can show what the record looked like when it was made, and what the model saw. At a few hundred thousand cases a year, there is no sharding story.

**Orchestration.** Each new case version starts a durable workflow, for example in [Temporal](/post/temporal-human-in-the-loop), keyed by case ID and version, so a message delivered twice runs once, and a question to a physician can wait days for its answer without a process waiting with it. Versions are debounced: ten lab results in an hour cause one re-run, not ten. Every detector, model and grouper result is cached on a hash of case version, catalog year and detector version, so a re-run only pays for what changed.

**Detectors.** Here I'd split recall from precision. Cheap, high-recall signals run on every version: a lab value over a threshold, a positive nutrition screening, supplement drinks on the medication list, a procedure code without a matching diagnosis. They are code, fast, and free to run every time. Each candidate becomes a few fixed questions for the evidence selector. The documents are split into numbered passages, a search keeps the twenty most relevant to the candidate, and a decision model answers: which of these passages proves it, or none; was it treated; did a physician diagnose it.

A non-generative decision model, the kind [Jev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jev_(AI_model)) made popular, cannot write text at all: given a question and a fixed set of options, it returns a choice or a calibrated probability, [typed](/post/pydantic-ai-type-safe-hybrid-workflows) like any other step. That suits this job better than a generative LLM. A citation chosen from numbered passages can't point at a quote that isn't there, which is a better story for an audit than checking generated quotes afterwards. And the probabilities give the thresholds for suggesting, asking and dropping, and the acceptance probability the worklist ranks by.

A rule layer then encodes the mechanical DKR checks: is this code combination valid, does D003 hold given what the evidence says was done.

**Grouper.** The certified grouper as a service: case in, DRG and revenue out, deterministic and fast, milliseconds per run. To price a suggestion it runs the case twice, with and without the code.

**Worklist.** Ranked by expected value: revenue delta times the probability the coder accepts, minus a penalty for audit risk. A code worth nothing still belongs on the case, since the guidelines ask for every relevant diagnosis, so it ranks low rather than vanishing. Each suggestion shows the quote highlighted on its page, the DRG before and after, and one-click reject reasons. Suggestions that need one fact only the physician can supply become a question instead, bundled per case: one yes or no per case, not six interruptions.

**Write-back and labels.** Accepted codes go back into the KIS through the same adapter, so the hospital's system stays the record of truth. Many installations don't allow writing codes back at all, though; there the first version is a coder copying the code, and that is fine. Decisions go to a labels table the evaluation reads.

## The life of one suggestion

<DesignMap preset="suggestion" />

*Highlighted: the part where the model, the rules and the money meet.*

The kidney failure takes the easy path: the creatinine threshold fires, the selector picks the lab report and the note pausing the ACE inhibitor, D003 holds, and the grouper prices the move to F62B. It becomes a suggestion in the cardiology coder's queue. The malnutrition is harder, and more typical:

<DesignFigure name="suggestion" />

*The malnutrition case, from the signal that raised it to a question for the physician. Dropping is dashed: it is the outcome nobody sees.*

The screening score of 4 is the cheap signal. The evidence selector reads the passages from the scanned nursing notes and picks the dietitian's note on page 1, with the weight loss and the supplement drinks. The rule check says D003 holds, since the malnutrition was treated, but finds no physician's diagnosis, which a coder needs. So it doesn't become a suggestion. It becomes one bundled question to the physician: "Moderate malnutrition, relevant to treatment? Dietitian's note attached." If they confirm, it re-enters the queue as a suggestion, priced like any other. Had the signal been a single low potassium value, normal on the recheck and never treated, D003 would fail and the candidate would be dropped.

The tradeoffs in this part:

- **Rules, a model, or both.** Rules alone are precise but brittle: the creatinine rule works, but nobody writes a rule for every way a nurse records weight loss. A model alone needs a question to answer, and asking it about every possible code on every case version buries the coder in low-confidence guesses. The hybrid uses rules to say *where to look* and the model to *read what's there*. That matches [a support automation I built](/post/first-level-support-automation): code in charge of the sequence and the model confined to interpretation made each piece testable on its own.
- **Which model.** An open-weight decision model, self-hosted in a German data center; hosted decision models run outside Germany, which rules them out for patient data. Models of this kind are new and their benchmarks are their makers' own, so I'd measure one on a few hundred coder-labelled candidates before trusting it. The fallback is a generative open-weight LLM in the same data center, slower and costlier but well understood. The selector's typed interface makes the model a swap, not a redesign.
- **Precision over recall.** A coder who sees three bad suggestions in a row stops trusting the queue, and then the good ones go unread too. I'd start with a few detectors that are nearly always right, and add more only when the acceptance rate holds.
- **How to search the counterfactuals.** Running the grouper once per suggestion is fine for a handful. Exploring every clinically plausible variant of a case can mean thousands of runs, which brute force still handles in seconds at milliseconds per run, with caching per case version. Smarter would be to exploit the grouper's structure: most codes don't touch the decision path that matters for this DRG, so only the ones near a decision boundary need varying. Cases near a boundary are interesting both ways: upside if a documented fact is missing, audit risk if a claimed one is thin.
- **Workflow or agent.** For this pipeline, a [workflow](/post/workflow-vs-agent): every suggestion goes through the same checks in the same order, and the order is what makes it defensible in an audit. An agent might earn a place later, for open-ended work like drafting an answer to an MD query.

When to upgrade: let the decision model ask about codes no signal raised only once the signals' misses are measured and coders still trust the queue, and move off brute-force counterfactuals only when grouper runs show up in the time it takes a case to reach the queue.

## Running it: evaluation, rollout, security

<DesignMap preset="finishing" />

*Highlighted: the decisions, which close the loop into the evaluation.*

**Evaluation.** Coder decisions are the labels: cheap, fast and plentiful, but biased by whatever the queue showed them. MD audit outcomes are the real ground truth, months later and only for the few percent of cases that get audited. And an acceptance rate is not recovered revenue: what counts is the euros that survive an audit, the same gap as [between an approval rate and savings](/post/approval-rate-is-not-savings). Coders also disagree with each other, so I would measure each reviewer's agreement with the others before trusting their labels. Offline replay is weaker than it looks, because it is hard to recreate what the record looked like at the moment a coder would have seen the suggestion. Running new detectors in shadow mode on live cases, logging what they would have suggested, is the honest test. [The Missing Test Suite](/post/the-missing-test-suite) makes the longer case for that, and for why the experts' review throughput is the real bottleneck.

**Adding a hospital.** A new adapter, or a configuration of an existing one, a per-hospital overlay for house rules on top of the shared DKR rules, and a backfill of open cases. The yearly catalog updates for ICD-10-GM, OPS and the DRG catalog mean every rule, signal and grouper run is tied to a catalog year, versioned and tested like code. A case admitted in December and discharged in January is the edge case to test first.

**Security.** German hosting only, a model that runs there too, encryption, per-hospital isolation, and the case store's audit log, which shows which data the model saw for each suggestion.

**Contracts and growth.** The three records from the simplest design, case version, suggestion and decision, are the contracts between the parts, so ingestion, detectors, the grouper service and the review UI can change independently. They also carry the next products: answering MD queries from the same evidence, billing privately insured patients under the GOÄ (*Gebührenordnung für Ärzte*), the physicians' fee schedule, and showing controlling the revenue at risk.
