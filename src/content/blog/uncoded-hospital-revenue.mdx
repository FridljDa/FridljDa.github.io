## TL;DR
German hospitals are paid per case, by codes. When something was treated and documented but never coded, the hospital simply doesn't get paid for it. "Design a system that fixes that" is a nicely vague problem, and this post works it the way I approach any vague design problem: settle the requirements, sketch the simplest design that works end to end, make each box concrete, dig into the tradeoffs of the part that matters most, and finish with evaluation, rollout and security. The map of the system starts as a black box and gains detail with every section. The result, in one line: cheap signals find candidate codes, an LLM only confirms them and must cite the page, a certified grouper prices them, and a coder decides. The hard parts are not throughput but integration, trust and evaluation.

## The approach: narrow down step by step

I narrow a vague problem down in five steps, each more concrete than the last, and use them as this post's sections:

1. **Requirements.** Who uses it, at what scale, under which constraints.
2. **A simple end-to-end design.** Abstract and technology-agnostic, with the data that flows between the steps.
3. **Components.** Turn each abstract box into concrete building blocks, with the reason for each.
4. **Tradeoffs.** For the part that matters most, the options, what each costs, and when to upgrade.
5. **Finishing touches.** Evaluation, rollout to the next customer, security, how the work splits across a team, and how the system grows.

Start simple, and only add complexity when something forces it. And keep the people in view: here they are coders with a queue, physicians with no time, and a Medizincontrolling department that answers to management for every euro.

## How a German hospital gets paid

You can't design a system for a domain you can't draw, so first the chain the system has to fit into.

<DesignFigure name="billing-chain" />

*The six stations of an inpatient case's billing. The system in this post sits between the first two.*

A hospital is paid per inpatient case (*Fall*), with a flat rate called a DRG. Physicians and nurses document the treatment in the hospital information system (KIS, for example Dedalus ORBIS, iMedOne or SAP i.s.h.med). Coders (*Kodierfachkräfte*) turn that record into codes: ICD-10-GM for diagnoses, one main diagnosis and any number of secondary ones, and OPS for procedures. The rulebook for that is the DKR, the German coding guidelines. A certified grouper, deterministic software whose catalog InEK publishes each year, then maps the codes plus age, length of stay, ventilation hours and a few more fields to a DRG. The invoice is roughly the DRG's cost weight times a state base rate of 4,000 to 5,000 EUR.

Secondary diagnoses matter more than you'd think. They feed a severity score (PCCL, 0 to 6) that often decides the DRG's suffix letter, and F62B pays more than F62C. But under DKR D003, a secondary diagnosis only counts if it caused treatment, diagnostics or extra care. The other levers are ventilation-hour thresholds, length-of-stay limits (deductions below the lower limit, surcharges above the upper one), the choice between outpatient, inpatient and Hybrid-DRG, and add-on payments.

The invoice goes to the patient's Krankenkasse, which can send it to the Medizinischer Dienst (MD) for audit. The MD cuts whatever the record doesn't support. Until the end of 2026, payers may audit 5 to 15 percent of a hospital's invoices. From 2027, a hospital that has more than 35 percent of its audited invoices objected to can face audits of up to 40 percent. With two thirds of general hospitals running at a loss in 2024, that makes two things matter at once: finding the revenue that was earned but not coded, and never claiming a code the record can't defend.

That second half is the one this design keeps coming back to. A system that only maximises revenue is an upcoding machine, and the 2027 rule punishes exactly that.

## Step 1: Requirements

<DesignMap preset="clarify" />

*All we know so far: documentation goes in, and a coder should get something to act on.*

The running example is invented. A 74-year-old is admitted with heart failure. On day three, the lab report shows creatinine at 4.3 mg/dl, more than three times the baseline: acute kidney failure, stage 3. The ward treats it and documents it. Nobody codes it. N17.93 would have moved the case from F62C to F62B, worth around a thousand euros. Multiply that by every case in every department.

Before drawing anything, the problem needs boundaries. These are the ones I'd settle on:

- **Coders act on the output.** They own the codes, so they get the suggestions. Physicians are the source of missing facts, and controlling only sees totals.
- **Review runs while the patient is admitted and settles by discharge.** Earlier leaves time to ask a physician while they still remember the case; later gives a more complete record. Concurrent review gets both.
- **A wrong code costs more than a missed one.** A missed code costs its revenue delta once. A wrong code can fail an audit, and from 2027 a bad audit record raises the audit rate for every case. So the system optimises for precision.
- **The data is heterogeneous.** Structured data from the KIS over HL7 v2 or FHIR, discharge letters, lab PDFs, scanned nursing notes, in a different mix at every hospital.
- **Patient data stays in Germany.** GDPR and the hospitals' own rules mean German data centers, and an LLM that runs there too.
- **Scale is small.** A hospital has roughly 20,000 to 80,000 inpatient cases a year, a few hundred discharges a day. Even a few dozen hospitals are nothing for a database.

The last point reshapes the whole design. This is not a system where the hard part is requests per second. The hard parts are getting data out of a dozen KIS installations, keeping the view of a case up to date as the record changes, and making coders trust suggestions enough to act on them. That decides where the rest of this post spends its time.

## Step 2: A simple end-to-end design

<DesignMap preset="end-to-end" />

*Six steps, still technology-agnostic. Blue boxes are the platform, the teal one is a person, the grey ones belong to the hospital.*

The simplest design that works end to end:

1. **Hospital systems** send what happens to a case.
2. A **case context store** keeps one record per case: every code, document and lab value, with the time it arrived.
3. **Gap detectors** read a case and propose codes it is missing, each with the evidence for it.
4. A **counterfactual grouper** runs the case with and without each proposed code and reports the revenue delta.
5. A **ranked worklist** shows the coder the suggestions worth their time, best first. They accept or reject.
6. **Write-back and labels:** accepted codes go back into the KIS, and every decision goes back to the detectors as a label.

The data between the steps is small and worth naming, because it is the interface teams would build against:

- a **case version**: case ID, hospital, version number, codes, documents and lab values as of that version;
- a **suggestion**: case ID and version, proposed code, evidence (document, page, quote), the rule that allows it, the revenue delta, and a status (open, accepted, rejected, asked);
- a **decision**: suggestion ID, who, accept or reject, and a reason from a short list.

Two things already fall out of this. Suggestions point at a case *version*, so when the record changes we know which suggestions to re-check. And the grouper sits after the detectors, not inside them: the LLM proposes, deterministic certified software does the money math. I never want a model to tell a coder what a code is worth.

## Step 3: Components

<DesignMap preset="components" />

*The same six steps, each with the components that make it concrete.*

Now each box becomes building blocks.

**Ingestion.** One adapter per KIS, translating HL7 v2 messages, FHIR resources or file drops into one internal case timeline. Admission, transfer, new lab result, new document, discharge: each is an event appended to the case. Documents go through OCR when they are scans. This is the least glamorous component and the one I'd expect to eat the most engineering time, because every hospital's installation is configured differently.

**Case store.** An append-only event log per case, with a materialised current view. Versioning comes for free from the log, and so does the audit log: for every suggestion we can show what the record looked like when it was made, and what the model saw. A relational database handles this at a few hundred thousand cases a year without any sharding story.

**Detectors.** Here I'd split recall from precision. Cheap, high-recall signals run on every new event: a lab value over a threshold (creatinine over 3x baseline), a medication that implies a diagnosis (insulin without a diabetes code), a procedure code without a matching diagnosis, ventilation hours near a threshold. They are code, fast, and free to run on every version. Only the candidates they raise go to an LLM, whose job is narrow: find the passage in the documentation that supports or refutes the candidate, and cite the document and page. A rule layer then encodes the DKR checks that are mechanical: is this code combination valid, does D003 hold given what the evidence says was done.

**Grouper.** The certified grouper as a service: case in, DRG and revenue out, deterministic. To price a suggestion it runs the case twice, with and without the code.

**Worklist.** Ranked by expected value: revenue delta times the probability the coder accepts, minus a penalty for audit risk. Suggestions missing one fact the physician can supply become a question instead, bundled per case: one yes or no per case, not six interruptions.

**Write-back and labels.** Accepted codes go back into the KIS through the same adapter, so the hospital's system stays the record of truth. Decisions go to a labels table the evaluation reads.

## Step 4: The life of one suggestion

<DesignMap preset="suggestion" />

*Highlighted: the part where the model, the rules and the money meet.*

Following the running example through it:

<DesignFigure name="suggestion" />

*The kidney-failure case, from the signal that raised it to one of three outcomes. Dropping is dashed: it is the outcome nobody sees.*

The new lab result arrives and the threshold signal fires: creatinine 4.3 mg/dl, more than three times baseline, and no N17 code on the case. The evidence extractor finds the lab report, page 2, and a later note that dialysis was ordered. The rule check confirms D003 holds, since the kidney failure was treated, and the grouper says N17.93 moves the case from F62C to F62B. The evidence is complete, so it becomes a suggestion in the cardiology coder's queue, with both pages linked. Had the record shown the lab value but no treatment, it would have become a question to the physician: "Acute kidney failure, relevant to treatment? Lab report attached." Had the grouper said the code changes nothing, it would have been dropped.

The tradeoffs in this part:

- **Rules, LLM, or both.** Rules alone are precise but brittle, and nobody writes a rule for every way a physician phrases "we treated it". An LLM alone reads everything but is expensive on every version of every case, and its mistakes are hard to explain. The hybrid uses rules to say *where to look* and the LLM to *read what's there*. That matches what I saw in my own work on a support automation: code in charge of the sequence, the model confined to interpretation, made each piece testable on its own.
- **Precision over recall.** A coder who sees three bad suggestions in a row stops trusting the queue, and then the good ones go unread too. I'd start with a few detectors that are nearly always right, and add more only when the acceptance rate holds.
- **How to search the counterfactuals.** Running the grouper once per suggestion is fine for a handful. Exploring every clinically plausible variant of a case can mean thousands of scenarios. Brute force works at that size if each run is fast and results are cached per case version. Smarter would be to exploit the grouper's structure: most codes don't touch the decision path that matters for this DRG, so you only need to vary the ones near a decision boundary. Cases near a boundary are interesting both ways: upside if a documented fact is missing, audit risk if a claimed one is thin.
- **Workflow or agent.** For this pipeline, a [workflow](/post/workflow-vs-agent): every suggestion goes through the same checks in the same order, and the order is what makes it defensible in an audit. An agent might earn a place later, for the open-ended work like drafting an answer to an MD query.

When to upgrade: add an LLM-based recall stage only once the cheap signals' misses are measured and worth the cost; move off brute-force counterfactuals only when grouper calls show up in the latency budget.

## Step 5: Finishing touches

<DesignMap preset="finishing" />

*Highlighted: the decisions, which close the loop into the evaluation.*

**Evaluation.** Coder decisions are the labels: cheap, fast and plentiful, but biased by whatever the queue showed them. MD audit outcomes are the real ground truth, months later and only for the few percent of cases that get audited. Coders also disagree with each other. In my support automation, approval rates ranged from 37 to 100 percent depending on the reviewer, on the same system, so I would measure each reviewer's agreement before trusting their labels. Offline replay is weaker than it looks, because you can't easily recreate what the record looked like at the moment a coder would have seen the suggestion. Running new detectors in shadow mode on live cases, logging what they would have suggested, is the honest test. [The Missing Test Suite](/post/the-missing-test-suite) makes the longer case for that, and for why the experts' review throughput is the real bottleneck.

**Onboarding hospital number 20.** A new adapter, or a configuration of an existing one, a per-hospital overlay for house rules on top of the shared DKR rules, and a backfill of open cases. The yearly catalog updates for ICD-10-GM, OPS and the DRG catalog mean every rule, signal and grouper call is tied to a catalog year, versioned and tested like code. A case admitted in December and discharged in January is the edge case to test first.

**Security.** German or EU hosting only, an LLM that runs there too, encryption, per-hospital isolation, and the audit log from step 3 that shows which data the model saw for each suggestion. Certifications like ISO 27001 and BSI C5 are table stakes for hospital customers.

**Evolution.** Once the case context store exists, the same view of a case powers the next products: drafting answers to MD queries from the same evidence, GOÄ billing for privately insured patients, and controlling dashboards on revenue at risk.

**Team split.** Ingestion and adapters; the case store; detectors and rules; the grouper service; the review UI; and evaluation. The interfaces between them are the three records from step 2, which is why I'd name them early.
